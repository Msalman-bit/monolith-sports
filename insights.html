What GPSR actually changed for importers of sporting goods

The General Product Safety Regulation, (EU) 2023/988, has applied since 13 December 2024. It replaced the old General Product Safety Directive, and the practical difference for anyone importing sports equipment is larger than the name change suggests.

The three things that actually bite

You need an economic operator in the EU. A product cannot be placed on the EU market unless there is a manufacturer, importer, authorised representative or fulfilment service provider established in the Union who is responsible for it. If you import from Pakistan, that is you, unless you appoint someone. Their name and address must appear on the product or its packaging.

You need a technical file, and you need to hold it. Risk assessment, a description of the product, test reports, and a record of the standards applied. It must be kept for ten years and produced to a market surveillance authority on request. A supplier's assurance email is not a technical file.

Traceability has to be real. A type, batch or serial number on the product itself, not just the carton. When a recall happens you need to be able to say which units are affected and where they went.

What this means when you are choosing a supplier

Ask three questions before you place an order. Can you send me the technical file for this SKU today? Who is your named EU responsible person? Where is the batch code physically printed? A factory that has done this work will answer in a single email. A factory that has not will ask you what you mean.

The obligation sits with the EU importer, not the Pakistani manufacturer. Your supplier can make it easy or hard for you to comply, but they cannot comply on your behalf.

Online marketplaces

If you sell through Amazon, Kaufland, bol.com or similar, the marketplace is now obliged to collect and display the responsible person's details, and to remove listings that lack them. Sellers who did not have this in place discovered it through delistings rather than through correspondence. If your listing is currently suppressed for a missing GPSR contact, appointing a responsible person and re-submitting usually resolves it within days.

Hand-stitched or thermally bonded: choosing by use case, not price

Buyers often treat thermal bonding as the premium option and hand stitching as the budget one. That is roughly true on price and roughly wrong on suitability.

What bonding actually buys you

Thermal bonding eliminates needle perforations. Water cannot enter through stitch holes, so the ball does not gain weight in the rain. It also produces a more consistent surface, which matters for flight predictability. This is why match balls are bonded and why FIFA's water uptake test exists.

What it costs you is repairability and, more importantly, tolerance of abuse. A bonded seam that fails, fails completely. A stitched seam that starts to go can be repaired, and usually degrades gradually.

A practical decision table

  • Senior match play, competitive leagues. Thermally bonded. The water uptake difference is decisive on a wet Saturday.
  • Academy and club training, high volume. Hand or machine stitched. They will be kicked against walls and left in nets, and stitched construction survives that better per euro spent.
  • Indoor futsal. Stitched, with a damped bladder. Bonding buys you nothing indoors and the low-bounce spec is the real variable.
  • Promotional and giveaway. Machine stitched, two or three ply. Anything more is money spent on performance nobody will measure.
  • Youth sizes 3 and 4. Either, but check EN 71-3 compliance first. That matters more than construction at this age.

The provenance argument

There is one more consideration that has become commercially real. A hand-stitched ball from a monitored Sialkot centre carries a story that a machine-bonded ball does not, and several European retailers now price against that. If your brand positioning leans on craft and traceability, the stitched line is not the cheap option — it is the product.

FOB Karachi or DDP Rotterdam: where the risk really sits

Incoterms allocate three separate things, and most disputes come from assuming they move together. They do not. The rules allocate cost, risk and the obligation to arrange carriage — and under several terms these split apart.

The classic trap: CIF

Under CIF the seller pays for freight and insurance to the destination port. It feels like the seller carries the goods to the destination. They do not. Risk transfers when the goods are loaded on the vessel at origin. If the container goes overboard in the Arabian Sea, the loss is yours, and you are claiming on an insurance policy that we bought and that meets only the minimum cover required by the rule.

If you want the seller to carry the risk into Europe, you need a D-term: DAP or DDP. There is no C-term that does this.

Why we usually recommend FOB or DAP

FOB Karachi gives you control of the main carriage. If you have a freight contract, your rate will almost always beat ours, and you can consolidate with other suppliers. You take risk from the ship's rail, which your marine policy should already cover.

DAP your DC gives you a single delivered price with no freight management, while leaving import clearance, duty and VAT with you — which is correct, because reclaiming import VAT is straightforward for you and awkward for us.

DDP looks simplest and is often the worst value. It requires us to act as importer of record, which means we need an EU VAT registration and we price the risk of getting your customs classification wrong. That risk premium is usually larger than what your own broker charges.

Ask for the same order quoted FOB, DAP and DDP. The gaps between those three numbers tell you exactly what your logistics function is worth.

One more thing about EXW

EXW puts export clearance on the buyer, and a foreign buyer generally cannot file a Pakistani export declaration. In practice the seller ends up doing it anyway, outside the terms of the contract and without clear liability. If you want origin-side control, use FCA instead. It does what people think EXW does, properly.
